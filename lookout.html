MARKET INDICATOR

Lookout asks whether an AI can read a stock’s move better than history can.

Every trading day a fixed scanner flags S&P 500 stocks that moved unusually: a large company-specific move, a volume spike, an earnings reaction or a material filing. Claude studies the top five and predicts whether each will beat or lag its sector ETF over the next 5, 10 or 20 sessions. Every call is logged before the close and scored when its horizon ends. Nothing is traded.

The bar is close to a coin flip.

From 2010 to 2025, after the same kinds of moves, 44–53% of S&P 500 stocks went on to beat their industry over the next 5–20 sessions. Claude has to beat that base rate, and a fixed checklist built from published research, on the same calls before any of this is used with real money.

Hit rate is the share of calls where the stock moved the way the call said, measured against its sector ETF. Skill is 1 minus Claude’s Brier score over the base rate’s; above zero is better than history, and it rewards honest confidence as well as direction.

Who predicts better

The checklist scores every flagged stock with fixed, literature-based adjustments (momentum, one-month reversal, post-earnings drift, 52-week-high proximity, idiosyncratic volatility, high volume). Random controls are unflagged stocks scored the same way, which tests whether flagging itself carries information.

Does Claude size moves right?

Each call also estimates how far the stock will move against its sector if the call is right. This compares those estimates with what actually happened, in the called direction. It is descriptive only and never counts toward a gate, but it matters for options: each spread’s strikes are set from this estimate.

What would the calls earn as trades?

The calls predict a stock beating or lagging its sector, but a small account would usually just buy the stock. This shows each settled call as a $250 position three ways, after fixed costs (10 bp per leg round trip, 0.5%/yr borrow on shorts). The checklist on the same calls is the comparison. Drawdown is the worst run of losses across positions, and adverse is the worst close while a position was held. Descriptive only, never a gate.

How it works, and limits
  1. ScanThree times a day on delayed data, each move is split into market, sector and company parts.
  2. FlagFixed rules, set in advance, pick unusual names. Five random names are also scored as controls.
  3. Fact sheetThe same fifteen or so fields for every name: the move split, trend, momentum, earnings timing, filings, volatility and macro.
  4. PredictThe checklist scores every flag. Claude takes the top five and must explain where it disagrees with the checklist.
  5. LogCalls go into a hash-chained ledger before the close, so none can be edited later.
  6. ScoreWhen the horizon ends, each call settles against its sector ETF with dividends included.

Limits

Model judgment is only tested forward, because any backtest would overlap the model’s training data. The historical base rates use an industry benchmark rather than sector ETFs. There is no news feed, so Claude sees only the numbers and filings in the fact sheet. Results need at least 60 resolved calls before they mean much.
