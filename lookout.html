MARKET INDICATOR

Lookout asks whether an AI can read a stock’s move better than history can.

Every trading day a fixed scanner flags S&P 500 stocks that moved unusually. As of November 6, the bet being tested is a narrow one: a stock that jumped at least 2% on unusually high volume, and the day was not an earnings report. A fixed rule says that stock will lag its sector over the next month. Claude still reads up to five of those names a day, and that opinion is scored on its own. Nothing is traded.

The bar is close to a coin flip.

From 2010 to 2025, after the same kinds of moves, 44–53% of S&P 500 stocks went on to beat their industry over the next 5–20 sessions. A call has to beat that base rate, and a fixed checklist, before any of this is used with real money.

What changed on November 7

Nothing about the live test changed. The rules, the prompt and the pass/fail lines are the same. The work went into measuring the test more honestly and building a stricter challenger.

  • Cleaner prices. A stock’s move is no longer compared with an ETF move from a different day. A stock with mismatched data is skipped and the skip is logged. This was written into the rules before any call finished.
  • Clearer open calls. Each open call now shows the stock’s own return, the sector ETF’s return, and a beta-adjusted return, so a call that “wins” only because the ETF fell is easy to spot.
  • Honest sample size. Calls made on the same day often share one market bet. With a real 7-point edge, 60 calls would pass the test only about 4 times in 10, and about 230 are needed to be fairly sure. Passing at 60 means more paper testing, not money.
  • A stricter Claude (v3), now on. It reads the same stocks as the current version, must cite a filing for every factual claim, sees the last earnings release with its date, and may answer “no edge”. Those answers are still scored. Its rules were frozen on November 7 and it starts with the next trading day’s run. Its main rival is the free volume rule on the same stocks.

What changed on November 6

A check of S&P 500 history from 2010 to 2025 found one pattern large enough to test live. When a stock rises at least 2% on about three times its normal volume, and that day is not an earnings report and not an even bigger company-specific shock, the stock has usually lagged its industry by about 1% over the next 20 trading days. The typical case was almost the same as the average, so a few crashes do not explain it. Ordinary up days, without the heavy volume, did not lag. About 1,100 cases, 14 of 16 years.

That is history. No live call on this page has finished yet, so the pattern is not a result. History of this kind often shrinks once you start keeping score.

  • The rule. Every stock that fits the volume pattern gets a paper bet that it will lag its sector. The confidence is fixed. This bet does not ask Claude.
  • Claude, from here. The five daily reads are spent only on those volume-spike stocks. They start from zero toward the pass/fail lines.
  • Claude, already made. The first fifteen calls were earnings days, and every one of them pointed the same way as that day’s move. They still settle and stay on the page. They no longer count toward the pass/fail lines.
  • Busy days. A day with more than 15 “company-specific” flags is marked. The scanner may be calling a market-wide shock fifteen different company stories. Those days are labeled, not thrown out.

The last of the current volume bets finishes its 20 trading days on December 7. The page is the place to read whether the live bets look like the history.

Hit rate is the share of calls where the stock moved the way the call said, measured against its sector ETF. Skill is 1 minus Claude’s Brier score over the base rate’s; above zero is better than history, and it rewards honest confidence as well as direction.

Who predicts better

The checklist scores every flagged stock with fixed, literature-based adjustments (momentum, one-month reversal, post-earnings drift, 52-week-high proximity, idiosyncratic volatility, high volume). Random controls are unflagged stocks scored the same way, which tests whether flagging itself carries information. “Follow the move” uses Claude’s own confidence but always bets in the direction of the move that triggered the flag, so it differs from Claude only where Claude went against the move.

Versions, side by side

Each version of the Claude analyst is scored on its own, and only one experiment counts toward the gates: E2 until November 10, E4 from November 11. E3 (v3) was frozen on November 7 and starts with the next trading day’s 15:25 run. It reads the same stocks as E2, right after E2, with sourced filings, an older earnings release marked as background, beta-adjusted moves and an options-implied move. It is allowed to say “no edge”, and those answers still count in its probability score. E4 was added on November 10. An audit that day found that most live volume-up flags fire on a mid-session volume projection and do not end the day at the three-times-normal volume the historical study tested, so E4 re-checks the event at 15:25 on the study’s own definition and starts counting from zero. E2 runs on unchanged. On November 11 the real-money gates moved from E2 to E4, before any call of either had settled. E4 must also beat the fixed fade rule on the same stocks. A test of the same volume rule on mid and small caps failed its preset bar, so the scan stays on the S&P 500.

Does Claude size moves right?

Each call also estimates how far the stock will move against its sector if the call is right. This compares those estimates with what actually happened, in the called direction. It is descriptive only and never counts toward a gate, but it matters for options: each spread’s strikes are set from this estimate.

What would the calls earn as trades?

How it works, and limits
  1. ScanThree times a day on delayed data, each move is split into market, sector and company parts.
  2. FlagFixed rules, set in advance, pick unusual names. Five random names are also scored as controls.
  3. Fact sheetThe same fifteen or so fields for every name: the move split, trend, momentum, earnings timing, filings, volatility and macro.
  4. PredictThe checklist scores every flag. A volume spike also gets a fixed “lag the sector” bet. Claude is asked only about volume spikes, up to five a day.
  5. LogCalls go into a hash-chained ledger before the close, so none can be edited later.
  6. ScoreWhen the horizon ends, each call settles against its sector ETF with dividends included.

Limits

Model judgment is only tested forward, because any backtest would overlap the model’s training data. The volume rule is the exception that was measured on history, with the pass rules written down first, and it still has to repeat on the live page. The historical base rates use an industry benchmark rather than sector ETFs. There is no news feed and no analyst-estimate feed yet; a free news feed is being set up to be measured in shadow (stored, not shown to Claude) before any experiment uses it. Results need a finished sample before they mean much. The first gate is still 60 resolved calls.
